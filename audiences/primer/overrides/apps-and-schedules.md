# Build it once, run it every week

Apps, schedules and projects.

notes:
Skills make one task repeatable. This section is about the next step: a small app the whole team uses, work that runs on a schedule, and projects that let a team share the same setup.

<!-- id: describe-an-app -->
## Build the app you wish you had by describing it

- Say what goes in, what comes out, and what it checks
- Example: a quote app. Form in, document out, checklist before review
- It runs inside Claude, with your connectors and your memory
- Share it with the team, then improve it as you use it

notes:
I built my email app on a Sunday night by talking to my phone in bed: here's what I want my inbox to look like. It built it. Every time something annoyed me the next day, I asked for a change. You can do the same for quoting. Describe the form, the document it should produce, and the checks it should run before anything goes to review. The app uses everything Claude already has: your Drive, your CRM, your memory.

## A pre-send checklist catches what tired people miss

### 01 | Billing contact
On file, and it matches what the CRM says.

### 02 | PO and tax ID
Present whenever this customer needs them.

### 03 | Names, dates, prices
Consistent across the quote and the agreement.

### 04 | Tax and terms
Applied by the customer's location and renewal terms.

notes:
This is the part that pays for itself. Every quote that bounced back from review bounced for one of these reasons. Write them down once, make them the app's checklist, and it runs every time, at eleven at night, without getting tired. When a policy changes, like a new renewal discount, you update the checklist in one sentence and everyone gets it.

## Research, plan, then build

### Research
Ask for the background on the account, topic or problem.

### Review
Read it. Fix what's wrong before anything builds on it.

### Plan
Ask for an outline: the slides, the emails, the screens.

### Build
Now make the deck, the campaign or the app.

notes:
"Make me a deck" works sometimes. For anything with nuance, and selling infrastructure software is all nuance, it's faster to go in this order. Have it research first. Read the research and correct it. Ask for a plan and argue with the plan. Then build. You spend five minutes up front instead of an hour spinning on a draft that started from a wrong assumption.

## Schedule the prep. Keep the send.

### 1st of the month | Customers to contact in the next 90 days
A list with reasons and drafts, sent to you for review.

### Every Monday | Follow-ups you owe
Pulled from email, call notes and the CRM.

### Every morning | Inbox triage
Replies drafted and waiting before you sit down.

notes:
Anything you do on a calendar can run on a schedule. The first of the month, it builds the list of customers you should be talking to in the next ninety days, with the reason for each one and a draft. Monday, it lists the follow-ups you owe. Every morning, it drafts replies. It does all the prep. You read it and you send it. Once a scheduled job has been right for a month or two, you can decide how much rope to give it.

## Use a project when the team shares the work

### One per workflow
A Quoting project, a Demo prep project, a Renewals project.

### Shared instructions
The same rules and templates for everyone in it.

### Shared chats
Your teammate's best run is there for you to reuse.

notes:
A project groups the chats, files and instructions for one job. Make a Quoting project, put the templates and the checklist in it, and everyone who quotes works from the same setup. When someone nails a demo script or a tricky renewal, that chat lives in the project and the next person starts from it. Pin the projects you use every day so they don't get buried.

# It knows how to sell software. It doesn't know how to sell *yours* until you tell it.

Prompt engineering was the old job. Context engineering is the new one.

notes:
Think of Neo in The Matrix downloading kung fu. Claude already has the manual for selling software, writing a quote, running a discovery call. What it doesn't have is your flight path: your product, your customers, your pricing rules, the white paper you quote every week. Save those in the shared Drive, point Claude at them, and trim them every quarter. That's the whole game.
