## 06 · good-at · layout cards · module 01-where-ai-fits

where ai fits
What it's good at today, and what it isn't
Good at
Reading and summarizing
A 10-K, forty job postings, a two-hour transcript. Faster than you, and it doesn't get tired. It does miss things, which is why the sources matter.

Good at
First drafts and rehearsal
Email, follow-ups, proposals, and playing the skeptical buyer so you can practice.

Not yet
Knowing your buyer
It has never met them. It doesn't know what was said at the last QBR unless you tell it.

Not yet
Judgment and relationships
When to push, when to walk, who actually signs. That's still you, and it's the paid part.

Notes: Keep this honest. The tools are good at volume and language. They are bad at truth they haven't been given and at judgment. Think of a very well-read new hire on their first day: eager, fast, and knows nothing about your accounts until you tell them. Everything we do today is about telling them.

## 13 · three-habits · layout body · module 02-three-files

briefing
Three habits that fix most bad answers

- Give it the source material. Paste the page, the transcript, the job post. Don't make it guess

- Ask for three options, then pick and refine one. Never accept the first draft

- Tell it what to check. "Flag anything you're not sure is true" works better than hoping

Notes: Most complaints about these tools come down to under-briefing. People type nine words and get a generic answer, then decide it doesn't work. Feed it, ask for options, make it show its doubts. That's it. Demo one bad prompt and one good prompt live if time allows.

## 17 · account-brief-prompt · layout body · module 03-research

research · the prompt
The account brief prompt
prompt · copy this
Build a one-page account brief on [Company]. Use my ICP and product files.
Sources attached: [paste or link site, news, job posts, filings, contact profiles].

Sections: what they do and how they make money; what changed in the last six months;
what they're hiring for and what that says; who my contacts are and what they care about;
where Linkerd plausibly fits, in their words; three discovery questions; one "why now" sentence.
Cite a source for every factual claim. If you can't source it, mark it UNVERIFIED.

Notes: Read the last line out loud. UNVERIFIED in capital letters is the whole trick. The model will tell you what it's unsure of if you give it permission. Then the brief becomes a to-do list for your own checking instead of a trap.
I'll paste each prompt in the chat as I run it; they're all in the repo. Nobody needs to transcribe a slide.

## 19 · what-to-check · layout body · module 03-research

research · what to check
Four things it gets wrong, and how you catch them

- Stale people. Titles change. Check the two contacts on LinkedIn before you mention them

- Look-alike companies. Two firms with similar names get blended. Confirm the domain

- Invented numbers and quotes. If you can't click to it, it didn't happen

- Trigger events that are old news. Check the date on anything it calls recent

Notes: These four cover almost every embarrassing research mistake I've seen. They take ninety seconds to check and they are the difference between sounding prepared and sounding like you used a bot. Which is exactly what the buyer is listening for right now.

## 22 · first-touch · layout body · module 04-outreach

outreach · the first touch
Under ninety words, one ask, an easy out
prompt · copy this
Using my voice file and the account brief for [Company], write a first-touch email to [Name, Title].
Rules: under 90 words, plain text, no bullet points, no buzzwords.
Open with the trigger from the brief in one sentence. Ask one plain question about what they're
trying to solve. Offer the docs or 20 minutes, their choice. Close with a line that makes it easy
to say no. Give me three versions with different openings.

Notes: Ninety words is a real constraint and the model respects it. The easy out matters: "if this isn't on your radar, tell me and I'll stop" gets replies because it costs the reader nothing. Three versions, then you pick and tune. Never the first draft.

## 24 · before-after · layout two-col · module 04-outreach

outreach · before and after
The same email, before and after the brief
Before, from a cold template

Hi Sarah, I hope this finds you well. I'm reaching out because Buoyant helps organizations like yours unlock value with Linkerd. Leading companies trust us to drive outcomes. I'd love to find 30 minutes to share how we can help Acme accelerate its journey. Are you free next week?

After, from the brief and the voice file

Sarah, your team posted four platform roles last month, which usually means the Kubernetes footprint just got bigger than one person can watch. What are you trying to fix first with it?

Happy to point you at the right docs, or 20 minutes if talking is easier. If it's not on your radar, say so and I'll stop.

Notes: Read both out loud. The left one could have been sent by any company to any company. The right one proves homework in the first sentence, asks one real question, and makes "no" a free answer. Say up front that the left one was written to be bad on purpose; every phrase in it is from a real vendor email. Same model wrote both. The difference is what it was given.

## 25 · sequences · layout body · module 04-outreach

outreach · sequences
What to automate in Apollo and what stays in your hands

- Three emails, same thread, days 0, 3 and 8. The last one asks if you should close it out

- LinkedIn stays a manual task. Automating connection requests gets your account restricted

- Stops on reply, meeting, bounce, unsubscribe, or an opportunity. Nurture pauses while they're in it

- The AI drafts every step. You approve the first send to each person

Notes: This is the sequence shape I use. Email can be automated once the drafts are approved, and every automated send carries a working unsubscribe and your postal address; Apollo handles both if the template is set up right. LinkedIn cannot be automated, and it's not a style choice; the platform restricts the member's account for it. The approval on first send is the human gate. Later, when the reply and complaint numbers look healthy, loosen it.

## 30 · prep-prompt · layout body · module 05-calls

the call · prep
The prep sheet prompt
prompt · copy this
Using the account brief for [Company] and my product file, prepare me for a 30-minute discovery call
with [names and titles]. Give me: five open questions in the order I'd ask them; the two objections
I'm most likely to hear and a one-line honest answer to each; what each person in the room probably
cares about; the two technical terms from the brief I should be able to explain in one sentence;
one thing I should not bring up yet. Keep it to one page I can read in five minutes.

Notes: "One thing I should not bring up yet" is my favorite line in this prompt. Pricing, a competitor, a feature that isn't shipped. The model is good at spotting the landmine when you ask. The two technical terms line is for anyone selling to engineers: if you can't explain the buyer's words in one sentence, they'll know in the first five minutes.

## 31 · roleplay · layout body · module 05-calls

the call · rehearsal
Rehearse the hard conversation before you have it
prompt · copy this
Play [Name], [Title] at [Company]. You're skeptical, busy, and you've been burned by a vendor
like us before. Use the account brief for what you know and care about. I'll pitch and handle
objections; you push back the way a real buyer would, one message at a time. Don't go easy on me.

Notes: Nobody wants to role-play with their manager. Everyone will do it with a chatbot at eleven at night. The feedback at the end is honest in a way colleagues rarely are. Managers: this is your new ride-along.

## 35 · four-rules · layout cards · module 08-guardrails

guardrails
Four rules
01
Some things never go in
Customer confidential data, personal data beyond a name and title, unreleased pricing or roadmap. Unless your company's tool is approved for it, it stays out.

02
Use the company seat
The team or business account has the retention and training settings your admin set. Your personal login doesn't.

03
Check before you trust
Names, numbers, dates, quotes. If you'd be embarrassed to be wrong about it, click the source.

04
It never sends
A working unsubscribe and your postal address on every sequence, by law. The assistant drafts. A person reads and sends.

Notes: Rule one is the one people break by accident, usually by pasting a whole transcript with a customer's architecture in it into a free consumer tool. Ask your security team which tool is approved and what can go in it. Rule two is the fix for most of rule one. Rule four has a legal half: every commercial email needs a working unsubscribe and a real postal address, and the sequencer handles both if the template is set up right. Check with ops before the first send. The other half you've heard all session.

## 36 · compounding · layout statement · module 08-guardrails

guardrails
At 99 percent accuracy, a thousand automated sends is ten mistakes with your signature on them.
Errors compound at machine speed. The human gate is the circuit breaker.

Notes: This is why the one rule exists. I'm not claiming models are 99 percent accurate; even if they were, the math still gets you. A model that's right 99 times out of 100 feels perfect in a demo. Run it unattended across a thousand contacts and the ten bad ones are the ones that get forwarded. Keep the person in the loop where the name goes on it.
