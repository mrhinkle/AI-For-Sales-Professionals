### 01. AI for Sales Professionals

_Opening_

Welcome. Say the time budget out loud and the promise.
By the end you will have run three real prompts on your own accounts and you'll know which one to do tomorrow morning.
Housekeeping: questions any time, I'd rather go off script than leave you behind.

### 02. I've been on both sides of this table

_Opening_

One story, under a minute. The point to land: I'm not a vendor selling you an AI tool. I use these things to sell, and I've watched three platform shifts. Every time, the people who won weren't the earliest adopters. They were the ones who understood what the thing actually was before they bet their week on it.

### 03. The AI drafts. You send.

_Opening_

Say it twice. This is the rule that keeps the whole thing safe and useful at the same time. It isn't because the tools are bad. It's because it's your name and your relationship. If you remember one thing, remember this one.

### 04. Here's how we'll spend the time

_Opening_

Walk the agenda in thirty seconds.
Flag the hands-on bits so people get a real account ready. Ask them to open the assistant they already use. Any of them is fine for today.

### 05. About half of a seller's week isn't selling.

_Where AI fits in a seller's week_

Ask for a show of hands: who spent more than an hour yesterday on research, drafting, or CRM updates? Everybody. That's the target. We are not replacing the part where you build trust and read a room. We're buying that part more hours.

### 06. What it's good at today, and what it isn't

_Where AI fits in a seller's week_

Keep this honest. The tools are good at volume and language. They are bad at truth they haven't been given and at judgment. Think of a very well-read new hire on their first day: eager, fast, and knows nothing about your accounts until you tell them. Everything we do today is about telling them.

### 07. Your week, with an assistant in the chair next to you

_Where AI fits in a seller's week_

This is the map for the rest of the session. Every row is a module. The pattern is the same in every one: you give it the source material, it drafts, you check and send. Point, don't read.
We'll do the top rows hands-on.

### 08. You probably already own the tools

_Where AI fits in a seller's week_

Resist the urge to buy anything this quarter. The general assistant plus a recorder gets you ninety percent of what we'll cover. The CRM copilots are good at the boring parts, which is exactly where you want them. Tool shopping is procrastination with a budget.

### 09. Set it up once

_Set it up once, the three files_

This is the module people skip, and it's the one that makes everything else work. Twenty minutes, once.

### 10. Three files your assistant reads before every task

_Set it up once, the three files_

Plain text, each under a page. Keep them in whatever your assistant calls a project or a folder, so they load automatically. The voice file is the one people underestimate. Give it your real emails and it stops sounding like a brochure. The ICP file is what makes research and list-ranking useful. The product file keeps it from inventing features.

### 11. Put them where the assistant reads them every time

_Set it up once, the three files_

Show the folder feature in whichever assistant the room uses. The point is one-time setup: you never paste the three files again. If someone's company tool has no projects feature, a pinned chat with the three files pasted at the top works, it's just slower.

### 12. Brief it like a new hire, not like a search box

_Set it up once, the three files_

Five lines. Who you are, who it's for, what you want, what good looks like, what to avoid. Then the magic line: ask me questions first. A new hire who asks three good questions before starting beats one who guesses. Same here.
Have everyone paste this into their assistant now and fill in the brackets for a real account.

### 13. Three habits that fix most bad answers

_Set it up once, the three files_

Most complaints about these tools come down to under-briefing. People type nine words and get a generic answer, then decide it doesn't work. Feed it, ask for options, make it show its doubts. That's it. Demo one bad prompt and one good prompt live if time allows.

### 14. Build your voice file right now

_Set it up once, the three files_

Six minutes. Phones away, open the sent folder, find five. If they can't find five they're proud of, three is fine. Walk the room. The test email at the end is the check: if it doesn't sound like them, they tell it what's off and run it again. Save the result as voice.md.

### 15. Research an account in ten minutes

_Research an account in ten minutes_

This is the fastest payback in the whole session. Most reps either skip research or spend an hour on it. Ten minutes, every time, is the goal.

### 16. Collect, brief, verify, then one sentence on why now

_Research an account in ten minutes_

Collect is where most of the ten minutes goes, and it's mostly copy and paste. If your assistant can browse, let it, but still paste the job postings. Hiring is the best public signal of what a company is about to do. Verify is non-negotiable. The brief is a draft, not a fact sheet, until you've clicked the links.

### 17. Six places to collect from, in the order that pays

_Research an account in ten minutes_

Point, don't read. The order matters: job postings first because they're the freshest signal, your own CRM last because it's the one people forget and the one that's most embarrassing to miss. All six links are in the repo.

### 18. The account brief prompt

_Research an account in ten minutes_

Read the last line out loud. UNVERIFIED in capital letters is the whole trick. The model will tell you what it's unsure of if you give it permission. Then the brief becomes a to-do list for your own checking instead of a trap.

### 19. Four things it gets wrong, and how you catch them

_Research an account in ten minutes_

These four cover almost every embarrassing research mistake I've seen. They take ninety seconds to check and they are the difference between sounding prepared and sounding like you used a bot. Which is exactly what the buyer is listening for right now.

### 20. Pick a real target account and run it

_Research an account in ten minutes_

Use the Prospector companion exercise in program/PROSPECTOR.md, or the account-brief prompt if the plugin is unavailable. Eight minutes with a timer on the screen. Walk the room and look for people stuck on Collect; help them grab the job postings. The debrief is the "why now" sentences. Push back gently on vague ones. If it isn't something you'd say on a call, it isn't done.

### 21. Outreach that sounds like you

_Outreach that sounds like you_

Everyone in the room has received AI-written outreach this week. They can smell it. The goal is the opposite: email that reads like a person who did ten minutes of homework.

### 22. Personalization isn't their name in the first line. It's proof you did the ten minutes.

_Outreach that sounds like you_

"Hi Sarah, I saw you're the VP of Platform at Acme" is not personalization. It's mail merge. "Your team posted four Kubernetes roles last month" is. The research module feeds this one directly.

### 23. Under ninety words, one ask, an easy out

_Outreach that sounds like you_

Ninety words is a real constraint and the model respects it. The easy out matters: "if this isn't on your radar, tell me and I'll stop" gets replies because it costs the reader nothing. Three versions, then you pick and tune. Never the first draft.

### 24. The same email, before and after the brief

_Outreach that sounds like you_

Read both out loud. The left one could have been sent by any company to any company. The right one proves homework in the first sentence, asks one real question, and makes "no" a free answer. Say up front that the left one was written to be bad on purpose; every phrase in it is from a real vendor email. Same model wrote both. The difference is what it was given.

### 25. What to automate in your sequencer and what stays in your hands

_Outreach that sounds like you_

This is the sequence shape I use. Email can be automated once the drafts are approved, and every automated send carries a working unsubscribe and your postal address; your sequencer handles both if the template is set up right. LinkedIn cannot be automated, and it's not a style choice; the platform restricts the member's account for it. The approval on first send is the human gate. Later, when the reply and complaint numbers look healthy, loosen it.

### 26. Nothing goes out that you didn't read. Not because the AI is bad. Because it's your name.

_Outreach that sounds like you_

Say it plainly and move on. People remember the short version.

### 27. Write the first touch for the account you just researched

_Outreach that sounds like you_

The read-aloud test is the best editor in the room. If they stumble on a phrase, the buyer will too. Collect two volunteers to read theirs and ask the room: would you reply to that?

### 28. Before, during and after the call

_Before, during and after the call_

The call is where the relationship happens, so the assistant stays out of the room. It earns its keep on either side of it.

### 29. Five minutes before, nothing during, ten minutes after

_Before, during and after the call_

Ask for consent on recording every time; most teams already do. The "during" box is the point: the best sellers in the room already don't take notes. Now nobody has to. After the call, ten minutes, before you open the next tab, or the follow-up slides to tomorrow and the energy is gone.

### 30. The prep sheet prompt

_Before, during and after the call_

"One thing I should not bring up yet" is my favorite line in this prompt. Pricing, a competitor, a feature that isn't shipped. The model is good at spotting the landmine when you ask. The two technical terms line is for anyone selling to engineers: if you can't explain the buyer's words in one sentence, they'll know in the first five minutes.

### 31. Rehearse the hard conversation before you have it

_Before, during and after the call_

Nobody wants to role-play with their manager. Everyone will do it with a chatbot at eleven at night. The feedback at the end is honest in a way colleagues rarely are. Managers: this is your new ride-along.

### 32. The recorder is the only new tool most of you need

_Before, during and after the call_

Any recorder works: the one built into Zoom, Teams or Meet, or a standalone one like Fireflies or Gong. What matters is that you stop typing during the call and that the transcript lands somewhere approved. Consent is a sentence: "I'm recording so I can pay attention instead of taking notes, okay with you?"

### 33. Follow up in ten minutes from the transcript

_Before, during and after the call_

The structure for CRM notes is whatever your team uses. Change the six headings to match your CRM. The contradiction flag at the end is the quiet win: it catches the moment the buyer told you something that changes the deal and you were too busy listening to write it down.

### 34. Three rounds with your toughest buyer

_Before, during and after the call_

Keep it to six minutes. People get into this one. The debrief question is the useful part: name the fix. If you want a bigger version of this, pair people up and have one person's assistant play the buyer while the other pitches live.

### 35. Proposals and the paperwork

_Proposals, business cases and the paperwork_

Proposals and RFPs are where sellers lose evenings. This is the module that gives them back, with one hard rule about numbers.

### 36. First draft from your template and the call

_Proposals, business cases and the paperwork_

Two protections are built in. Prices and dates stay as TBD so the model never invents a number that ends up in front of a buyer. And the last line catches the thing proposals are famous for: a promise that sales made and the product doesn't keep. Read every flag.

### 37. The same move works for RFPs and the business case

_Proposals, business cases and the paperwork_

The RFP library is a real asset once you have it. Twenty answered RFPs in a folder and the next one is a day's work instead of a week's. The business case: insist on shown math. If it can't show the arithmetic, it made the number up.

### 38. Keep the pipeline honest

_Keep the pipeline honest_

This is the module for anyone who has ever updated your CRM at 4:55 on a Friday. Also the one managers like most.

### 39. Ask it to be the skeptic in your deal review

_Keep the pipeline honest_

People find this uncomfortable and then they run it on every deal. It is very good at spotting the deal that's all enthusiasm and no economic buyer. Run it before the pipeline review, not after, so you walk in with the answer.

### 40. Hygiene without the Friday afternoon

_Keep the pipeline honest_

The weekly summary prompt takes two minutes and makes your manager's week easier, which is a career move dressed up as hygiene. Point at the fourth line: CRM copilots are good at structured summaries. The judgment prompts belong in your general assistant with your three files loaded.

### 41. Your CRM ships an assistant now. Give it the boring half.

_Keep the pipeline honest_

Every CRM is shipping agents this year. They're good at the structured work: summaries, field updates, follow-up reminders, because they sit on the data. They're generic at the judgment work, because they don't have your three files. Split it that way and you get the best of both.

### 42. The rules that keep you employed

_The rules that keep you employed_

Short module, serious tone. These aren't legal boilerplate. Every one of them is a story I've watched happen to someone.

### 43. Four rules

_The rules that keep you employed_

Rule one is the one people break by accident, usually by pasting a whole transcript with a customer's architecture in it into a free consumer tool. Ask your security team which tool is approved and what can go in it. Rule two is the fix for most of rule one. Rule four has a legal half: every commercial email needs a working unsubscribe and a real postal address, and the sequencer handles both if the template is set up right. Check with ops before the first send. The other half you've heard all session.

### 44. At 99 percent accuracy, a thousand automated sends is ten mistakes with your signature on them.

_The rules that keep you employed_

This is why the one rule exists. I'm not claiming models are 99 percent accurate; even if they were, the math still gets you. A model that's right 99 times out of 100 feels perfect in a demo. Run it unattended across a thousand contacts and the ten bad ones are the ones that get forwarded. Keep the person in the loop where the name goes on it.

### 45. Your first week

_Your first week_

Don't try to do all of this Monday. Pick one. The research brief is the usual winner because it pays off on the very first call.

### 46. Five days, one habit each

_Your first week_

Point, don't read. Each day is a module from today. By Friday they've done every workflow once on real accounts. Then they pick the one that paid off most and make it daily.

### 47. Measure three things, nothing fancy

_Your first week_

Three numbers on a sticky note. If hours back isn't at least three after two weeks, something's wrong with the setup, usually the three files. If the reply rate didn't move, the trigger line isn't landing. Same-day follow-ups is the one managers should watch.

### 48. Pick one workflow. Do it every day for two weeks. Then add the next.

_Your first week_

Last word before the close. The people who get value from this are the ones who make one thing a habit. The ones who try everything on Monday are back to the old way by Thursday.
