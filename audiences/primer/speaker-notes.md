### 06. Five steps, in this order

### 07. On day one it's a bozo. By day 90 it's doing real work.

_Teach it who you are_

Here's the honest expectation. The first week, it's going to miss things you think are obvious, because nobody told it. Every time you say "that was good, do it like that" or "no, we never say it that way," it gets better. The people who get great results aren't smarter prompt writers. They're the ones who kept correcting it for a month. Ninety days from now this will feel like a different tool, and it won't be the tool that changed.

### 08. Write your instructions by talking, not typing

_Teach it who you are_

Most people stare at the instructions box and type two lines. Don't. Click the microphone and talk for three minutes like you're onboarding a new hire: your role, what the company does, which system holds what, the links you paste ten times a week, what a good day looks like. Then ask Claude to clean it up and paste the result into Settings. You'll edit it later. The point is to stop re-explaining yourself in every chat.

### 09. Memory is its cliff notes on you. Check them.

_Teach it who you are_

If your admin turned memory on, Claude keeps notes as you work: how you write, who your customers are, what you're working on. That's what makes it proactive instead of reactive. The catch is that memory can go stale. If your minimum deal size changed and memory still has the old number, it'll keep using it. So once a quarter, open memory, read it, and delete or fix what's wrong. You can export it too. Fifteen minutes a quarter keeps it honest.

### 10. Give Claude your tools

_Give Claude your tools_

Everything after this depends on the next ten minutes. A chatbot that can't see your inbox, your calendar or your CRM is a very smart stranger. Let's fix that first.

### 11. Claude gives you superpowers. Only if you set it up.

_Give Claude your tools_

Most people open Claude, type a question, get a generic answer and decide AI is overhyped. That's like hiring a great rep and never giving them a CRM login. The setup is twenty minutes, once. After that every chat starts with your tools, your files and your rules already loaded. That's the difference between a toy and a teammate.

### 12. Turn on the connectors Claude already ships

_Give Claude your tools_

This is the directory. You get to it from Settings, then Connectors, then Browse. Click one, sign in with the account you already use, done. Start with email, calendar and your CRM. Those three alone change the research and follow-up we're about to do, because Claude can read the thread and the meeting instead of you pasting them in. Use your company account, and if IT controls connectors, ask them which ones are approved before you start clicking.

### 13. Decide what each tool is allowed to do

_Give Claude your tools_

Connectors run on MCP, the Model Context Protocol. Think of it as the USB port for AI: one plug, and any app that speaks it shows Claude its tools. Every connector, and the browser extension, lets you set each tool to always allow, needs approval, or blocked. Start strict. Let it read freely, make it ask before it changes anything, and block the things that can't be undone. Loosen up only as it earns trust.

### 14. Connect the tools you already sell with

_Give Claude your tools_

Here's the map for a seller. CRM first, because that's where the truth about an account lives. HubSpot has a native connector. Salesforce and Apollo you can reach through Composio, which is next. Email and calendar second. Then whatever you prospect with and whatever records your calls. If your company uses something not on this slide, the question is the same: does it have a connector, and if not, can Composio or the browser reach it.

### 15. Or do what I do: Composio, everything in one place

_Give Claude your tools_

This is what I run. Instead of connecting tools one at a time, Composio is one connector that brokers the rest, with the logins handled in one place. Their sales pitch is literally enrich a lead, draft the follow-up, book the meeting, across Salesforce, HubSpot, Gmail and LinkedIn. Native connectors are simpler and fine for most of you. Composio is for when your stack is bigger than the directory. Either way, company systems go through IT first.

### 16. Three files your assistant reads before every task

_Set it up once, the three files_

Plain text, each under a page. Keep them in whatever your assistant calls a project or a folder, so they load automatically. The voice file is the one people underestimate. Give it your real emails and it stops sounding like a brochure. The ICP file is what makes research and list-ranking useful. The product file keeps it from inventing features.

### 17. Put them where the assistant reads them every time

_Set it up once, the three files_

Show the folder feature in whichever assistant the room uses. The point is one-time setup: you never paste the three files again. If someone's company tool has no projects feature, a pinned chat with the three files pasted at the top works, it's just slower.

### 18. Skills are how you stop explaining yourself

_Teach it once with skills_

A skill is a set of instructions, sometimes with a template or a script, that Claude pulls in when the task matches. Anthropic's own description is folders of instructions, scripts and resources Claude loads when needed. The easiest way to make one is to ask: "Turn my voice file into a skill." Then you never paste it again, and it only loads when you're writing.

### 19. Turn a good chat into a skill

_Teach it once with skills_

You don't sit down to write skills. You do your job in Claude, and when something worked, you save it. Do the task in the chat so Claude sees every step. Get the output right before you save, because whatever's wrong gets passed down. Then one sentence creates the skill. Add success criteria, the way you'd brief an admin: every link works, nothing's cut off, never more than ten slides. Without them, it's "do your best," and sometimes its best isn't.

### 20. Context says what. A skill says how.

_Teach it once with skills_

A brand voice file is context, and it's valuable. A skill tells Claude what to do with it: copy the deck from the marketing folder, fill in the presenter name, save it to your folder instead of overwriting the original. And always add examples. A picture of a deck you love is worth more than a page of describing it.

### 21. Train it on your own sent mail

_Teach it once with skills_

This is the voice file, done for you. The skill reads about a dozen of your recent sent emails, skips the one-liners, and writes a short guide: how long you write, how you open and sign off, the words you use and the ones you never would. Read it, fix what's off, and from then on every draft starts from it. Out of the box, AI writes like a polite robot. Ten minutes of this and it writes like you.

### 22. Three skills every seller should build first

_Teach it once with skills_

These are the three files and two prompts you'll see today, packaged so Claude reaches for them on its own. Voice first, because every draft depends on it. The account brief is the research module in one skill. Follow-up is the one that saves the most time per week. Build one a day this week and you're done by Friday.

### 23. Make your skills learn from what works

_Teach it once with skills_

Here's a trick I use on every skill. Ask Claude to make it self-improving with a memory file. After each run it suggests what it learned, and you decide what to keep. A pitch skill learns which subject lines got opened and which got ignored. And when a policy changes, you update the skill in one sentence instead of retraining a team.

### 24. Build it once, run it every week

_Build it once, run it every week_

Skills make one task repeatable. This section is about the next step: a small app the whole team uses, work that runs on a schedule, and projects that let a team share the same setup.

### 25. Build the app you wish you had by describing it

_Build it once, run it every week_

I built my email app on a Sunday night by talking to my phone in bed: here's what I want my inbox to look like. It built it. Every time something annoyed me the next day, I asked for a change. You can do the same for quoting. Describe the form, the document it should produce, and the checks it should run before anything goes to review. The app uses everything Claude already has: your Drive, your CRM, your memory.

### 26. A pre-send checklist catches what tired people miss

_Build it once, run it every week_

This is the part that pays for itself. Every quote that bounced back from review bounced for one of these reasons. Write them down once, make them the app's checklist, and it runs every time, at eleven at night, without getting tired. When a policy changes, like a new renewal discount, you update the checklist in one sentence and everyone gets it.

### 27. Research, plan, then build

_Build it once, run it every week_

"Make me a deck" works sometimes. For anything with nuance, and selling infrastructure software is all nuance, it's faster to go in this order. Have it research first. Read the research and correct it. Ask for a plan and argue with the plan. Then build. You spend five minutes up front instead of an hour spinning on a draft that started from a wrong assumption.

### 28. Schedule the prep. Keep the send.

_Build it once, run it every week_

Anything you do on a calendar can run on a schedule. The first of the month, it builds the list of customers you should be talking to in the next ninety days, with the reason for each one and a draft. Monday, it lists the follow-ups you owe. Every morning, it drafts replies. It does all the prep. You read it and you send it. Once a scheduled job has been right for a month or two, you can decide how much rope to give it.

### 29. Use a project when the team shares the work

_Build it once, run it every week_

A project groups the chats, files and instructions for one job. Make a Quoting project, put the templates and the checklist in it, and everyone who quotes works from the same setup. When someone nails a demo script or a tricky renewal, that chat lives in the project and the next person starts from it. Pin the projects you use every day so they don't get buried.

### 30. It knows how to sell software. It doesn't know how to sell yours until you tell it.

_Build it once, run it every week_

Think of Neo in The Matrix downloading kung fu. Claude already has the manual for selling software, writing a quote, running a discovery call. What it doesn't have is your flight path: your product, your customers, your pricing rules, the white paper you quote every week. Save those in the shared Drive, point Claude at them, and trim them every quarter. That's the whole game.

### 51. The rules that keep you employed

_The rules that keep you employed_

### 52. Four rules

_The rules that keep you employed_

Rule one is the one people break by accident, usually by pasting a whole transcript with a customer's architecture in it into a free consumer tool. Ask your security team which tool is approved and what can go in it. Rule two is the fix for most of rule one. Rule four has a legal half: every commercial email needs a working unsubscribe and a real postal address, and the sequencer handles both if the template is set up right. Check with ops before the first send. The other half you've heard all session.

### 53. At 99 percent accuracy, a thousand automated sends is ten mistakes with your signature on them.

_The rules that keep you employed_

This is why the one rule exists. I'm not claiming models are 99 percent accurate; even if they were, the math still gets you. A model that's right 99 times out of 100 feels perfect in a demo. Run it unattended across a thousand contacts and the ten bad ones are the ones that get forwarded. Keep the person in the loop where the name goes on it.
